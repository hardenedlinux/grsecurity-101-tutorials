# OS exploit mitigations: a comparative history

**Scope.** Memory safety, control flow, isolation, and exploit mitigation on Windows, vanilla Linux (mainline kernel plus the glibc/GCC/Clang toolchain and typical distro defaults), OpenBSD, and PaX/grsecurity. Android, macOS/iOS, FreeBSD/HardenedBSD, Solaris, and compiler mitigations are included where they pioneered something the others later copied.

**As of.** 15 October 2026. A date is exact when a release note, man page, or named patch pins it. Years marked **by** or **~** are only a bound. Where two independent sources disagree and the document says so in the row, that disagreement is the finding, and the row names both.

**Verification pass, 15 October 2026.** Every dated and versioned claim was re-checked against at least two independent sources wherever one could be opened. `git.kernel.org`, `lore.kernel.org`, `lkml.org`, `man.openbsd.org`, and `man.netBSD.org` were unreachable, and `websearch` failed for part of the pass, so `kernel.googlesource.com`, the GitHub mirrors, raw vendor release notes, and a grsecurity patch archive were used instead. Corrections from this pass are listed at the end of the Sources section. What follows is what remains genuinely unsettled, not everything that was once uncertain.

**Still one source, or not re-opened.**

- *grsecurity/PaX.* KERNEXEC's read-only-GDT wording and the November 2005 module RX/RW split are Spender's own notes; the design itself is corroborated by the PaX docs index. The /proc options' "before 2002" and the "Aug 2007" auxv date have no located source. `PAX_USERCOPY`'s 27 Sep 2009 announcement is one mail, though the feature is in the 13 Aug 2009 patch. `RANDKSTACK` (Oct 2002), PAGEEXEC's 1 Oct 2000 first release, and PowerPC userspace ASLR (2003) are Wikipedia's, and the PaX material usually cited for them carries no dates. The December 2006 forum traffic behind UDEREF is unfindable: the forums search returns 403 and the public list archive only reaches back to 2011. "No grsecurity source connects AUTOSLAB to KERNSEAL", and the Voisin reconstruction could not be located, so KERNSEAL's beta and LTS dates are unconfirmed. grsecurity "from about 2001" rests on one page.
- *Windows.* The MSRC post of 12 June 2009 has no Wayback snapshot at any URL tried, so the user-mode half of the 64-bit DEP claim is unverified; the kernel-mode half is confirmed by the TechNet XP SP2 paper. High-entropy KASLR in Windows 8 (2012), partial kernel randomization in Vista and 7, the EMET-to-Exploit Guard lineage and its 1709 date, ACG as "Windows 10 (2015+)", and Server 2003/2008 defaulting DEP to OptOut could not be opened or refuted. CFG, CET, and kernel shadow-stack ship dates and KDP on 8 Jul 2020 were not re-opened. Memory integrity's default has two Microsoft pages that word it differently, "by default on clean installs of Windows 11" against "enabled by default on all new Windows 11 devices"; treat those as different claims.
- *Linux.* No `ZERO_CALL_USED_REGS` Kconfig symbol exists in any kernel from 5.15 to 7.2, so that name is either wrong or refers to a compiler flag. The 4.12 stack-canary widening is not a copy of PaX: its commit never mentions PaX. The PaX software-PAN dates for armv7 (4.3) and arm64 (4.10) rest only on the 2016 checklist. "6.6 through the end of 2026" traces to no announcement found, though it is stale: LWN (25 Feb 2026) gives 6.6 through end-2027. "6.18 chosen as next stable tree, 3 Dec 2025" is unsourced; 6.18.0 released 30 Nov 2025 and 6.18.1 was committed 12 Dec 2025. StackGhost's presence in OpenBSD 2.8 is not in 2.8's own notes. glibc framing claims (tcache reopening techniques, per-CPU cache entropy, safe-unlink being "incomplete") have no dated source.
- *Other platforms.* **FreeBSD NX in 5.3 is refuted, not merely unsourced** — see the row. Solaris and NetBSD are now resolved; see Sources. Apple: the per-SoC tables render checkmarks as images, so "M1–M4 have PAC" is unconfirmable from the guide, and every macOS OS-version date (10.5, 10.7, 10.8, 10.11, 10.14) has no reachable Apple source. "Exec Shield rejected as a mainline patch" has no located source. Hardened Gentoo's three claims are unverifiable, since grsecurity.net is now a commercial site. EMET's 2009 start is one secondary write-up and none of its four claims could be upgraded. "Windows 11" in the Binosi et al. finding is from the paper body, not its abstract.

**How to read the dates.** "Created" is the first design or shipping implementation, not the first academic ancestor. "Adopted" is the first release where the feature exists on that platform. Default-on is called out separately, because a merged config symbol is not a mitigation.

**Vanilla Linux** means mainline plus what Ubuntu, Debian, and Fedora actually enable. A Red Hat-only patch (Exec Shield) is not mainline. A KSPP-recommended config is not a distro default.

PaX and grsecurity were developed separately and shipped together. **PaX** (PaX Team, 2000) is the exploit-mitigation research line. Wikipedia dates the first release 1 October 2000, from an infobox with no cited source. The oldest dated PaX artifact found is `pageexec.old.txt`, 16 November 2000. **grsecurity** (Brad Spengler, from about 2001; one page states both "16 years" and "since 2001") is the patch set that includes PaX, plus RBAC, chroot hardening, and infoleak closure. Public patches stopped on **26 April 2017**. The commercial tree continues. The download page on 15 October 2026 lists stable trees **6.6.158** (dated 9 October 2026) and **6.18.56** (dated 12 October 2026), plus a **7.1.13** beta dated 9 October 2026. The 6.18 line is marked supported at least through Q4 2028. An earlier pin of 6.18.55 is already stale. The page's 6.6 row still reads "at least Q4 2026", which is itself stale: LWN, 25 Feb 2026, gives the upstream line as 6.6 through the end of 2027 and 6.12 and 6.18 through the end of 2028, and grsecurity has not updated that row. **Linux 6.18.0 was released 30 November 2025** and 6.18.1 was committed 12 December 2025. The earlier "6.18 chosen as next stable tree on 3 December 2025" date could not be sourced in this pass, and 6.18 did not become longterm when it was released. The historical public users were Hardened Gentoo for most of the project's life, and Alpine until the public patches ended.

## 1. Executable space, layout, and stack integrity

| Mitigation | Created / first implemented | Adopted in Windows | Adopted in vanilla Linux | Adopted in OpenBSD | Adopted in PaX/grsecurity | Primary purpose | Notes / limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NX / DEP / W^X / PAGEEXEC** (also XD, XN) | Hardware and tagged-machine precedents are much older (SPARC non-executable stacks via `noexec_user_stack` in Solaris 2.6, **August 1997**, but **disabled by default** and requiring an `/etc/system` edit). First practical IA-32 enforcement without an NX bit: **PaX PAGEEXEC, 1 Oct 2000** | **DEP**, XP SP2 and Server 2003 SP1. Client SKUs (XP, Vista) default to OptIn. Server 2003 and Server 2008 default to OptOut. On 64-bit Windows, 64-bit processes have had DEP always on and not disableable, well before Windows 8. The quoted sentence is attributed to an MSRC post of 12 June 2009 that could not be opened in this pass, and no Wayback snapshot of it exists. What a Microsoft primary source does support is the kernel-mode half: DEP is "always on" for drivers on 64-bit Windows and "cannot be selectively enabled or disabled" there (TechNet XP SP2 paper, Aug 2004). Client OptIn is confirmed; **Server 2003 and Server 2008 defaulting to OptOut was not confirmed by any Microsoft page found.** The August 2004 and 30 March 2005 calendar days remain unpinned, and Wikipedia's own DEP infobox says August 6, 2004, which differs from the commonly cited XP SP2 RTM date | Mainline: **32-bit PAE NX in 2.6.8** (14 Aug 2004), Ingo Molnár, modelled on Andi Kleen's x86-64 code. **x86-64 NX is earlier, in 2.6.7** (16 Jun 2004): the 2.6.7 ChangeLog carries a Kleen fix for a bug present "since NX is used by default in the kernel," and 2.6.6 has no NX at all. Wikipedia attributes x86-64 NX to 2.6.8, which the changelog contradicts. **Software-enforced non-exec stack from `PT_GNU_STACK` landed in 2.6.6** (Kurt Garloff). Exec Shield (Red Hat, 2 May 2003, not mainline) was a weaker CS-limit approximation: marking a high page executable could reopen everything below it |
| **PaX MPROTECT** (strict W^X policy) | **PaX Team, 2000**, the same year as PAGEEXEC. Their own LATINOWARE 2012 deck says "MPROTECT: runtime code generation control (in 2000)". The nearest dated primary artifact is `pageexec.old.txt`, 16 Nov 2000. A cited "secondary chronology" dating the first incarnation to November 2000 could not be identified in this pass. The design note file is dated 4 November 2003, which is the document, not the feature. No new executable anonymous mappings, no W+X file mappings, and `mprotect` cannot add execute to a writable mapping or write to an executable one | Closest product analogue is **ACG** (Arbitrary Code Guard), **opt-in per process** (Edge and some system processes). Not a system-wide default. Microsoft does not publish one ACG date: the Exploit protection reference dates Exploit protection from Windows 10 version 1709 (2016), while `PROCESS_MITIGATION_DYNAMIC_CODE_POLICY` lists a minimum supported client of Windows 8.1 | No default equivalent. Opt-in **`PR_SET_MDWE`** (Memory-Deny-Write-Execute) in Linux 6.3 (2023). systemd `MemoryDenyWriteExecute=` dates to systemd 231 (2016) and only later used that prctl. Android SELinux `execmem` is stricter, but it is a policy, not a kernel invariant | W^X is the policy, strict since 6.0. **`mimmutable(2)` and immutable mappings** were implemented **4 Dec 2022** and shipped in **7.3** (10 Apr 2023), per the 7.3 release notes. They go further: permissions on the main binary, ld.so, libc, and the main stack cannot be changed | **Origin, and still the strictest historical userspace policy.** Per-binary exceptions via `chpax` / `paxctl`. **EMUTRAMP** emulates a few legitimate trampolines so the policy can stay on | Stop a write primitive from creating a new executable region | Breaks JITs, some language runtimes, and GCC nested-function trampolines unless excepted. Linux desktops still allow `mprotect(PROT_EXEC)` by default. OpenBSD's exception is an ELF note plus a mount option, which is harder to flip accidentally than a per-process API |
| **ASLR** (userspace) | Term coined and first implementation by the **PaX team, July 2001** (`RANDUSTACK`, `RANDMMAP`, `RANDEXEC`). Design in PaX `aslr.txt` | **Vista** (RTM Nov 2006, GA Jan 2007), opt-in per image via `/DYNAMICBASE`. Heap, stack, PEB, and TEB also randomized. **Windows 8** added high-entropy 64-bit ASLR (`/HIGHENTROPYVA`). The linker option is documented but carries no version date on its Microsoft page, and no source tying it to Windows 8 or 2012 could be opened in this pass. Force-ASLR exists today as "Force randomization for images (Mandatory ASLR)"; the EMET lineage and the 1709 date are unconfirmed here | Weak mainline ASLR in **2.6.12** (17 Jun 2005), originally about 8 bits of mmap entropy, which is Wikipedia's figure for 32-bit. `randomize_va_space=2` is the usual distro default. Exec ASLR requires PIE. Entropy improved, then **regressed in 5.18** (May 2022): file-backed mappings of 2 MiB or larger, including modern glibc, became 2 MiB-aligned and lost 9 bits. Ubuntu raised `mmap_rnd_bits` in response. Binosi et al., ACM CCS 2024, confirmed the reduction but give no bit count. **The "9 bits" figure and Ubuntu's `mmap_rnd_bits` response are one source (Wikipedia ASLR).** | **3.4** (1 Nov 2003): libraries load in a random order, and i386 maps them at somewhat randomized addresses. The innovations page calls this the first widely used default ASLR. The release note is narrower than a modern ASLR. Library relink of `libc`, `libcrypto`, and `ld.so` was implemented May 2016 and is default from **6.0**, expanded in **6.2**. **sshd is relinked at boot from 18 Jan 2023.** fork+exec so privileged children do not inherit a parent's layout | **Origin.** i386 design figures in the PaX doc: 24 bits of stack, 16 bits of mmap, 16 bits of exec. Also the first to argue that ASLR without crash reaction is brute-forceable, because `fork` preserves the layout | Make code and data addresses unpredictable, so a write bug is not a reliable control-flow hijack | Any info leak collapses it. 32-bit entropy is brute-forceable if retries are cheap (Shacham et al., CCS 2004). Linux `offset2lib` (2014) showed a fixed executable-to-library delta on PIE. Binosi, Barzasi, Carminati, Polino, and Zanero, ACM CCS 2024 ("The Illusion of Randomness"), found Windows and macOS often randomize executables and libraries less thoroughly than Linux distros, and confirmed the 5.18 entropy drop. The arXiv abstract names "Windows and MacOS" without a version, so "Windows 11" comes from the paper body, not opened in this pass. PaX/grsecurity treated repeated crashes as a signal. Mainline does not |
| **KASLR / KARL / kernel-stack randomization** | PaX **`RANDKSTACK`, Oct 2002**: per-thread kernel *stacks*, not the kernel image. Image-base KASLR is a later, weaker idea. grsecurity's public position, March 2013 ("KASLR: An Exercise in Cargo Cult Security"), is that a single boot-time slide is not ASLR and is retired by one leaked pointer | Partial kernel randomization in Vista and 7. **High-entropy KASLR in Windows 8** (2012): kernel, HAL, drivers, and pools. Still leakable. **No source for the Vista/7 partial randomization or for the 2012 Windows 8 KASLR date was openable in this pass.** iOS KASLR (iOS 6, 2012) was called out in that 2013 post as about 8 bits, 2 MiB-aligned | Mainline x86 **`CONFIG_RANDOMIZE_BASE` in 3.14** (30 Mar 2014), a year after the grsecurity critique. Compile-time `default n`, not a boot-parameter opt-in. **Default-on in 4.12** (2 Jul 2017) unless `nokaslr`. Coarse: one slide of the whole image. The per-syscall kernel-stack offset landed in **5.13** (2021) as `randomize_kstack_offset=` and `CONFIG_RANDOMIZE_KSTACK_OFFSET_DEFAULT`. The unsuffixed `CONFIG_RANDOMIZE_KSTACK_OFFSET` symbol is **5.18**. Function-granular KASLR was proposed and is not the default | **KARL** (Kernel Address Randomized Link): relink the kernel from a link kit at every boot. Implemented June 2017, shipped in **6.2**. Randomizes *order inside* text and data, which is what a base slide does not do. The booted image is still fixed until the next boot. **6.3** adds a small random gap at the top of thread stacks. That is a few bytes, not KARL | PaX randomized kernel stacks in 2002, and userspace ASLR on PowerPC from 2003. Both dates are Wikipedia's; the Oct 2003 PaX deck the RANDKSTACK claim cites carries no dates at all, and `randkstack.txt` has none.  It did **not** treat image-base KASLR as a serious defense. Interior randomization in this tree is `RANDSTRUCT`, not a KASLR slide. AUTOSLAB is heap isolation, not layout randomization | Stop reliable kernel exploitation that needs absolute kernel addresses | A base slide falls to one kernel pointer leak, and the kernel image on disk is often public, so the only secret was the base. Side channels (prefetch, TSX, BTB, EntryBleed) leak it without a software bug. KARL raises the cost of relative assumptions. `nokaslr` remains a one-flag disable on Linux. grsecurity's 2013 post is the right caveat on every "KASLR is enabled" claim in this table |
| **KPTI / KAISER** (Meltdown) | The mitigation was named **KAISER**; the paper is *KASLR is Dead: Long Live KASLR*, Gruss, Lipp, Schwarz, Fellner, Maurice, Mangard, ESSoS **2017**, and it targeted KASLR leaks via side channels.  Became the Meltdown mitigation after CVE-2017-5754 (disclosed Jan 2018) | January 2018 updates (KB4056892 and follow-ups). Still present, with PCID optimizations. A permanent split of user and kernel page tables on affected CPUs | **Linux 4.15** (28 Jan 2018), backported widely. Default on affected x86 CPUs. Later side channels (EntryBleed and others) showed KPTI does not fully restore KASLR | **6.3** (April 2018). The release notes list a Meltdown mitigation for Intel amd64, and kernel page-table isolation on arm64 | UDEREF is not KPTI. UDEREF stops the kernel from *using* a user pointer. Meltdown leaks through a speculative access that never retires. grsecurity shipped its own Meltdown patches in the commercial tree; UDEREF alone was not the fix | Remove kernel mappings from user page tables so a Meltdown-class speculative load cannot read them | Performance cost on syscall-heavy workloads, reduced by PCID. Does not fix Spectre. Does not stop every KASLR side channel. CPUs with a hardware Meltdown fix do not need it, and the mitigation is often compiled out or disabled there |
| **Stack canaries / SSP / `/GS`** | **StackGuard**, Cowan et al., USENIX Security **1998**. **ProPolice / SSP**, Hiroaki Etoh (IBM), **2000–2001** | **`/GS` in Visual C++ .NET (2002)**. Windows system binaries rebuilt with it for **XP SP2 (2004)**. On by default in MSVC for many years. Later compilers reorder locals and cover more buffers | GCC **`-fstack-protector` in GCC 4.1** (2006). A kernel-stack canary option is in the **2.6.30** cycle. LWN, 1 April 2009, records it in that merge window. It was still default-off. The earlier "re-enabled 3 January 2009 for 2.6.29" date does not match a commit. `v2.6.29` still had the option marked broken. **`-fstack-protector-strong` in GCC 4.9** (2014). Mainline Kconfig still defaulted to none when strong mode was added in 3.14. Distro kernels later turned the strong kernel canary on: a Ubuntu 6.8 generic config and the Fedora 40 x86_64 kernel config both have `CONFIG_STACKPROTECTOR_STRONG=y`. The Ubuntu 6.11 OEM example has it too. Userspace toolchains on Ubuntu, Debian, and Fedora also default to a stack protector | **3.3 (May 2003): first OS to enable ProPolice system-wide.** Per-DSO cookies in 5.3. Largely superseded in base by **RETGUARD** | Not a PaX invention. The Feb 2017 RAP announcement compared full kernel RAP with SSP-all: similar cost, much less protection from the canary | Detect linear stack overflows that overwrite a saved return address | Usually one secret per thread or per DSO, not per function, unless the implementation says otherwise. An arbitrary read of the cookie, a non-linear overflow, or a bug that never crosses the canary all bypass it. Does not protect function pointers or the heap |
| **StackGhost** | **OpenBSD 2.8** (1 Dec 2000), SPARC register windows. OpenBSD 2.8 is confirmed by its own release page; **StackGhost's presence in 2.8 is not stated in that release's "What's New"**, and the grsecurity research page dates the paper to 2001. Frantzen and Shuey, USENIX Security 2001. Contemporary with PaX PAGEEXEC, and earlier than system-wide ProPolice | No SPARC register-window equivalent. CET shadow stacks are the later hardware backward-edge mechanism | No | **The first OpenBSD hardware-assisted stack protection.** Later RETGUARD is the portable software replacement on other architectures | No. PaX was x86-first | Use SPARC register windows so a stack overflow does not yield a usable saved PC | Architecture-specific. It does not transfer to x86. Included because "OpenBSD followed PaX" is false here: they had a hardware stack defense before they had W^X |
| **RETGUARD** | **OpenBSD**, Todd Mortimer. amd64 and arm64 in **6.4** (2018); mips64 in 6.7; powerpc in 6.9; amd64 syscall stubs in 7.3 | No direct equivalent. Hardware shadow stacks (CET) are the Windows backward-edge mechanism | No. Linux uses SSP, and CET shadow stacks where they are actually enabled | **Default in base** on supported architectures. Preceded by **trapsleds** (June 2017): incidental `NOP` / embedded `RET` sequences rewritten into traps | Different design from RAP. PaX/grsecurity went to type-based CFI instead of a cookie | Backward-edge integrity without a hardware shadow stack | Not a shadow stack. An arbitrary read of `.openbsd.randomdata`, or corruption that never hits the check, defeats it. The point of the design is that it works on every OpenBSD architecture, which CET does not. Trapsleds only remove accidental gadgets. They do not check returns |
| **RELRO / BIND_NOW** (GOT read-only) | GNU, Jakub Jelinek, **~2004** (`-Wl,-z,relro` and `-z now`). Partial RELRO protects the non-PLT GOT. Full RELRO needs immediate binding | No ELF GOT. Delay-load and the IAT are a different ABI | Partial RELRO became a distro default in the late 2000s. **Full RELRO (BIND_NOW)** is a current Ubuntu default toolchain flag. Debian enabled PIE in Stretch (2017); BIND_NOW followed. Not a kernel feature | **Stronger and earlier.** GOT and PLT made read-only outside ld.so in **3.3** (Dale Rahn, Theo de Raadt); `.init` / `.fini` in 3.4. **`kbind(2)`** was implemented July 2015 by Philip Guenther and shipped in **5.9** (March 2016). OpenBSD's own wording puts a person and a month in a release column | PaX did not implement ELF RELRO. MPROTECT plus non-executable data made a GOT overwrite less useful: a corrupted pointer still could not transfer into new code, and ASLR hid libc | Stop a GOT overwrite from becoming an arbitrary function-pointer write | Partial RELRO leaves the PLT GOT writable because of lazy binding. Full RELRO costs startup time. Useless against other function pointers (vtables, heap objects, return addresses). OpenBSD's version is system-wide. Linux still depends on how the binary was linked |
| **PIE** (position-independent executable) | Required for exec ASLR. PaX **`RANDEXEC`** (2001) randomized `ET_EXEC`. `ET_DYN` plus `RANDMMAP` became the preferred path. GCC `-fPIE` / `-pie` existed in the early 2000s | Windows images are not ELF PIE. `/DYNAMICBASE` relocation is the equivalent, opt-in from Vista, increasingly default in later MSVC linkers. Not every third-party EXE is linked that way | The kernel has long been able to load `ET_DYN` executables. Distro default is late: Fedora 23 (2015; the Fedora change page says PIE becomes the default for all packages then); **Ubuntu 16.10** (2016) on amd64, ppc64el, and s390x; **Ubuntu 17.10** on all architectures; Debian 9 (2017). Android required PIE for apps in **5.0** (2014) | Implemented Nov 2008 (Kurt Miller). Turned on in -current in August 2012 (Pascal Stumpf). **Shipped globally in 5.3** (May 2013), the first widely used OS to do so, on seven architectures. Static-PIE for `/bin` and `/sbin` in **5.7** (2015) | PaX depended on it. `RANDEXEC` was removed around late 2006 once `ET_DYN` was the expected format | Give the main executable the same randomness as a shared library | Without PIE, the executable base is fixed and return-to-text ignores ASLR. PIE has a real cost on register-poor 32-bit x86 (Ubuntu cited 5–10%), which is why 32-bit defaults lagged |
| **`_FORTIFY_SOURCE`** | Jakub Jelinek / Red Hat, **2004**. glibc plus GCC. Level 2 became the usual setting. **Level 3 arrived with glibc 2.33** (Feb 2021), which added `__builtin_dynamic_object_size` support to `_FORTIFY_SOURCE=3`. **GCC 12** (2022) added the matching Clang-compatible `__builtin_dynamic_object_size`. The two halves are separate, and the GCC 12 release notes do not mention `_FORTIFY_SOURCE` at all, so the level-3 pair is a glibc-then-GCC sequence | `/GS` and annotated `safeint` / SAL checks are the rough analogues, not this macro | Default in Ubuntu, Debian, and Fedora (`-D_FORTIFY_SOURCE=2`, sometimes 3). Object-size builtins, so it only helps when the compiler can see the destination size | No glibc. The local substitutes are `strlcpy` / `strlcat` (1 Jul 1998, released in OpenBSD 2.4 on 1 Dec 1998), `reallocarray` (22 Apr 2014, released in 5.6 on 1 Nov 2014), and a checked libc, not a fortify macro | Arjan van de Ven posted a kernel version in **2005** that did not land. PaX experimented in 2009 and did not merge it. The upstream feature, a separate implementation, landed in **4.13** (3 Sep 2017), Daniel Micay. kernelnewbies, Kees Cook's 4.13 notes, and the hardenedlinux checklist (commit `6974f0c4`) agree | Turn known-size `strcpy`, `memcpy`, `sprintf`, and similar calls into checked calls, and reject `%n` into a writable format string | Silent when the size is not visible at compile time. A wrapper that hides the buffer, or a heap object whose size is dynamic, is unprotected. It is a compiler assist, not a memory-safety model |
| **Stack-clash protection** | The bug class is older than the name. grsecurity's 14 Jul 2017 4.11 note says they had a **2010** kernel fix that did not break userland, and that they told Linus about the Java incompatibility in 2011. Qualys published Stack Clash in **June 2017**. GCC and Clang **`-fstack-clash-protection` in GCC 8** (2018) | Not this flag. Windows has its own stack probes | Current Ubuntu default compiler flag. Probes each page of a variable-length stack allocation. Kernel guard pages and `VMAP_STACK` (4.9, 2016) cover the kernel stack, not this userspace clash | Historical stack gap, then **`MAP_STACK`** (12 Apr 2018, release 6.4): a mapping is a stack only if it was created as one. **7.8** (22 Oct 2025) adds an unmapped guard page between the PCB and the kernel stack on 64-bit architectures with 4 KiB pages | grsecurity's 2010 fix is the early kernel-side claim. Not a PaX userspace compiler flag | Stop a stack allocation from jumping a guard page into another mapping | The compiler flag only covers binaries built with it, and only the clash case. `MAP_STACK` does not stop a pivot onto another legitimate stack. The 2010 grsecurity fix and the 2018 compiler flag are different mechanisms for the same bug class |

## 2. PaX/grsecurity kernel self-protection, and what others copied

This is the family that moved the industry. What mainline got after April 2017 is a subset, extracted from the last public 4.9 tarball or reimplemented from the design.

| Mitigation | Created / first implemented | Adopted in Windows | Adopted in vanilla Linux | Adopted in OpenBSD | Adopted in PaX/grsecurity | Primary purpose | Notes / limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **KERNEXEC** | **Since 2003.** Brad Spengler, 14 Jul 2017: the read-only GDT in PaX KERNEXEC dates to 2003, and upstream's later read-only GDT still left a globally writable map behind. **Module RX/RW separation added Nov 2005** | Partial analogue: SMEP plus kernel code integrity under HVCI / PatchGuard. No feature of this name. HVCI can make kernel code and page tables hypervisor-owned, which is stronger than KERNEXEC where HVCI is actually on | Never upstreamed as a policy. ### How late, and how completely, the copies arrived

| PaX/grsecurity idea | There | Analogue | Arrived | What was lost in the copy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Software NX on IA-32 | 2000 | Linux hardware NX; Windows DEP | 2004 | No NX emulator in mainline. Windows was opt-in for most apps |
| ASLR | 2001 | OpenBSD default; Linux mainline; Windows | 2003 / 2005 / 2007 | Entropy and brute-force reaction were not copied with the idea |
| MPROTECT | 2000 | Linux `PR_SET_MDWE`; Windows ACG | 2023 opt-in; 2016 opt-in | About 20 years, and still not a default. Microsoft gives no single ACG date |
| KERNEXEC | 2003 | SMEP plus a partially read-only kernel | 2011–2017 | The policy was not copied. Read-only text arrived as `STRICT_KERNEL_RWX` |
| UDEREF | ~2006 | Linux SMAP support; hardware around 2014 | kernel 2012 | PaX Team's "six years late" claim. Windows sets the CR4 bit on recent Windows 10/11 and still has no Linux-style policy as of October 2026 |
| REFCOUNT | Dec 2008 | `refcount_t` | 4.11 (2017) | The type was copied, the coverage was not. LWN, 24 Jul 2017: `refcount_t` "initially, protects far less code than the PaX-derived approach." Legacy `atomic_t` remained |
| USERCOPY | Aug 2009 | hardened usercopy | 2016 | Narrower whitelist |
| CONSTIFY | 2009–Nov 2011 | scattered `const`, never comprehensive | — | The maintenance, not the one-time patch, was the contribution |
| STACKLEAK | Apr 2011 | Linux 4.20 | 2018 | Copied, often left compiled out |
| RANDSTRUCT | Jan 2014 | Linux 4.13 plugin | 2017 | Copied, almost never enabled |
| RAP | Oct 2015, public from Apr 2016 | Clang CFI / FineIBT, not RAP | 2018–2023 | Different, narrower, and not a distro default |

Years in the "There" column are the first public patch containing the feature, read off the grsecurity patch archive, except ASLR and MPROTECT, which predate it. Where a mailing-list announcement is later than the patch, the patch date is used.

That pattern is the relationship. PaX shipped a software version of hardware that did not exist yet, and accepted compatibility breaks that a desktop OS with closed drivers and a JIT would not. Mainline later accepted the CPU bit (SMEP, SMAP, CET) or a reduced patch (usercopy, refcount, stackleak, random kmalloc caches), and dropped the part that broke drivers, JITs, or out-of-tree modules. KERNSEAL is the PaX design for sealed kernel storage. The design note is listed, unpublished, from 1 May 2003. AUTOSLAB, introduced in 2020, is an allocation-site isolation defense, and it does not seal pages or remove them from the direct map. **No grsecurity source documents AUTOSLAB as part of KERNSEAL**, so treat the two as neighbouring features rather than one design and a piece of it. Jann Horn's `SLAB_VIRTUAL` is a weaker public version of one piece, virtual non-reuse, and it is not in mainline.

## 7. What PaX/grsecurity actually was

The 2003 PaX design note already splits exploitation into three levels:

1. Introduce and execute new code.
2. Execute existing code out of the original order.
3. Execute existing code in order, with attacker-controlled data.

PAGEEXEC, SEGMEXEC, and MPROTECT answer the first. ASLR answers the second only probabilistically, and only if the attacker cannot read the layout and cannot retry forever. The project's second decade was the admission that levels two and three live in the kernel: KERNEXEC, UDEREF, USERCOPY, CONSTIFY, REFCOUNT, STACKLEAK, RANDSTRUCT, and finally RAP.

grsecurity owned the surroundings: RBAC, chroot and `/proc` restrictions, ptrace hardening, module-autoload lockdown, and the infoleak closures that make ASLR mean something. Spender's 12 March 2011 post, "The Unseen Benefits of a Security Mindset," is still the best primary document of the lag, and of the habit of copying a feature without copying the upkeep.

Three facts explain the adoption table.

**They shipped software versions of hardware that did not exist yet.** UDEREF is SMAP, six years early, built out of segmentation because there was no CPU bit. KERNEXEC is SMEP plus a policy, before SMEP. MPROTECT is ACG and `PR_SET_MDWE`, a decade or two early. RAP is CET-class CFI without CET. When the hardware arrived, vendors implemented the bit and treated that as the whole job. The policy that made the bit hard to turn off, and the coverage of kernel objects that are not code pages, stayed in the patch.

**They accepted breaks that a product OS will not.** MPROTECT breaks JITs unless the binary is excepted. RANDSTRUCT breaks out-of-tree modules. CONSTIFY breaks drivers that write their own ops tables. A desktop with a plugin ecosystem and a closed-source driver catalog will always choose the opt-in version. That is why Windows ACG, Linux MDWE, and CFG are per process or per binary. OpenBSD could be strict because it controls the userland, and it would rather add an ELF note and a mount option than weaken the default.

**The public patch ended before the upstreaming finished.** On 26 April 2017 the test patches, and all future PaX patches, became non-public. STACKLEAK made it in afterward only because Alexander Popov extracted it from the last 4.9 tarball and spent a year landing it. RAP, UDEREF-as-policy, KERNEXEC-as-policy, and SIZE_OVERFLOW did not. The KERNSEAL design note is listed from 2003 and was never published. AUTOSLAB arrived in the commercial tree in 2020. Neither was in a public tarball. The Kernel Self-Protection Project is the mainstream heir, and it is a subset. The community forks are also subsets:

- **linux-hardened** (Arch, after 2017) is a hardened config plus a small patch set. It is not PaX.
- **Dapper Secure Kernel Patchset** (Matthew Ruffell, 2017–2018) forward-ported the last public grsecurity test patch, **4.9.24**, onto the 4.9 stable series. The latest tagged release is **4.9.135**. From 4.9.75 the maintainer omitted Meltdown, Spectre, and retpoline, and both that note and the 4.9.135 note say to assume those fixes would be months, years, or never. The 4.9.135 note spells retpoline "Reptoline" It is a snapshot of the public patch, not the commercial tree, and it is not a 2026 defense.
So the live descendants in 2026 are a commercial grsecurity tree, a pile of mainline features that are individually weaker, and OpenBSD, which rarely took the code and often took the requirement. Dapper is a stalled 4.9-era port of the last public patch, not a fourth live tree.

OpenBSD's relationship to PaX is parallel invention under a different constraint, not a port. StackGhost (OpenBSD 2.8, December 2000) is earlier than W^X and is not a PaX feature at all, though 2.8's own release page does not list it. OpenBSD's innovations page claims W^X (3.3) and default ASLR (3.4) without attributing them to PaX. Chronologically PaX is first on IA-32 NX (October 2000) and on ASLR (July 2001). OpenBSD is first as a complete operating system that turned the ideas on for every process and then rewrote userland until the breakage stopped. From 2015 onward OpenBSD stopped following. pledge, unveil, KARL, RETGUARD, execute-only mappings, immutable address spaces, and pinsyscalls do not look like PaX features, and none of the other three platforms has the set. The 7.8 and 7.9 releases (2025–2026) are still tightening pledge and closing kernel-address leaks, not adding a new mitigation family. OpenBSD 8.0 is not released as of 15 October 2026. The release-notes page 404s rather than showing a placeholder; the placeholder is the errata page, which reads "None yet." The innovations list already tags `getexecpath` (31 Aug 2026) and `rtrd` (16 Sep 2026) for 8.0. Those are not exploit mitigations, and the list does not add a new mitigation family after pinsyscalls and `-fret-clean`.

## 8. Who led, and what is still open

**Historically.** PaX/grsecurity led the technical content from 2000 to about 2015: NX without hardware, ASLR, W^X as a policy, kernel/user separation, copy bounds, structure randomization, stack erasing, and the first serious kernel CFI. OpenBSD led deployment: the first general-purpose OS with stack protectors, W^X, and ASLR on by default, and the only one that kept tightening the default every release instead of adding a sysctl. Windows led productization at scale. DEP, `/GS`, ASLR, CFG, and HVCI reached hundreds of millions of machines, years after the idea, and usually opt-in for anything Microsoft did not itself compile. Linux mainline led almost nothing on this list. It integrated other people's work late, and distributors then chose which symbols to turn on. Android, and later Apple, led the hardware-assisted generation that PaX could not have built in 2001.

**Strongest default in late 2026.** It depends which default is meant.

- **An Apple device with MIE.** iPhone 17 and iPhone Air (A19, 9 September 2025), and **A19 and M5 or later** per the Apple Platform Security guide, whose current edition is dated **August 2026**. That includes M5 Macs. Always-on synchronous enhanced MTE on key attack surfaces, including the kernel, plus typed allocators and tag confidentiality. Nothing else here matches that against memory-corruption exploit chains on those chips. It is not every Apple device. Intel Macs and M1–M4 do not have it. Third-party apps are covered only if the developer opts in.
- **A general-purpose OS with one policy and no hardware lottery.** OpenBSD. Strict W^X, immutable mappings, ASLR, PIE, RETGUARD, execute-only text, pinsyscalls, KARL, pledge, and unveil, applied to the base system rather than to the subset of binaries that remembered the right linker flag. No memory tagging. Ports are less protected than base. Kernel CFI is not RAP. An M5 Mac is a general-purpose computer with stronger memory-safety hardware, and a weaker policy: third-party software is opt-in, and there is no pledge or pinsyscalls equivalent.
- **A corporate desktop, if the machine is a new Windows 11 device and HVCI stayed on.** Microsoft's own documentation says memory integrity is "enabled by default on all new Windows 11 devices." The 1 September 2026 IT Pro post says October 2026 quality updates will enable it on more eligible devices, and turn VBS on where needed, without overriding an existing off choice. The rollout is gradual. Add kernel CFG, kernel CET shadow stacks, PatchGuard, and user CET for Microsoft's own processes. That is a strong kernel. It is not every Windows install. Third-party software is often unprotected. The CR4.SMAP bit is on, on recent Windows 10 x64 and Windows 11. That is not a Linux-style policy, and the hole relative to Linux and OpenBSD on the same Intel CPU is that policy. A high-IRQL exception does not cover the syscall path.
- **A stock Linux server or desktop.** A solid 2010s baseline: NX, ASLR, PIE, full RELRO, strong stack protectors, FORTIFY, KASLR, SMEP, SMAP, KPTI, usercopy, and some init-on-alloc. The gap is exactly where PaX spent its second decade. No default MPROTECT, no default fine-grained CFI, coarse KASLR, a glibc heap that is merely decent, and user namespaces that add kernel attack surface. Android's generic kernel plus GrapheneOS is a different Linux, much closer to a productized policy. Commercial grsecurity is still the most complete Linux kernel self-protection stack, and almost nobody is allowed to redistribute the current patch.

**What is still open.** Info leaks and side channels still retire ASLR, and they have started to retire naive MTE. Every deployed CFI scheme is coarse enough that a call to a function of the right type, or a data-only attack, survives. JITs force a hole in W^X on every platform that runs a browser. Linux never grew a default equivalent of pledge plus pinsyscalls plus execute-only libc, and it never grew AUTOSLAB. Linux 7.2's typed kmalloc partitions are the closest mainline cousin, and they are Clang-only and not per call site. Same-size grooming still works on a stock distro kernel. `LIST_HARDENED` and `io_uring_disabled` exist, and the second still defaults to allowing io_uring. `SLAB_VIRTUAL`, Horn and Rizzo's 2023 proposal, is a weaker public version of one piece of that design: a virtual address is not reused for a different slab cache. It does not isolate per call site, does not seal, and does not hide. It is still not in the mainline hardening config. Userspace CET shadow stacks are compiled in and still mostly not enforced. Hardware mitigations are only as present as the oldest CPU the vendor still supports. Windows sets the CR4.SMAP bit on recent Windows 10 x64 and Windows 11, and still has no Linux-style policy. The 2020 MSRC study said a global clac-by-default enablement was not viable. Microsoft's driver documentation, updated 9 October 2025, still calls that policy a future feature. Yarden Shafir, 16 December 2024, said Microsoft was still working to bring it in. ESET (January 2022) and Connor McGarr (May 2022) disagree on the mechanism of the gap. They agree the syscall path is not the Linux policy. The October 2026 memory-integrity expansion is gradual, and it does not override an existing off choice. Pixel 11 firmware still force-disables MTE as of GrapheneOS's 2 September 2026 update. Speculative-execution mitigations are a treadmill, not a settled control. Memory-safe languages are removing code from the problem faster than new mitigations are appearing. The remaining C and C++ in kernels and browsers is still the surface these mechanisms were built for.

PaX defined the problem and the first real answers. OpenBSD is the only project that made those answers, and a second generation of its own, the default for a whole operating system. Windows and Linux shipped the compatible subset. Apple is the first to make hardware memory tagging a **default mitigation for general consumers** rather than a paper, a debug option, or a server-only feature: first on A19 phones, then on M5 and later. SPARC ADI shipped the same mechanism in Solaris 11.3 (2018) but only on Oracle's own SPARC servers, and never reached the x86 or ARM mass market.

KASLR therefore did **not** ship in NetBSD 8.0.
- Android kCFI vs Clang CFI: Android's kCFI documentation splits them by kernel version, Clang CFI for Linux 6.0 and lower because it relies on Clang LTO, and Clang kCFI for 6.1 and higher. Android developers blog, 10 October 2018, Sami Tolvanen: "Google's Pixel 3 will be the first Android device to ship with LLVM's forward-edge Control Flow Integrity (CFI) enforcement in the kernel." So a 2018 date belongs to Clang CFI only; kCFI is 2022 and later.
- Apple Platform Security, August 2026: PPL "Building on KIP and Fast Permission Restrictions", and "In the A15 or later and M2 or later SOCs, SPTM (in combination with TXM) replaces the PPL." KTRR and CTRR appear in neither the January 2026 nor the August 2026 edition, and are anachronistic for a 2026 statement.
- GrapheneOS, Mastodon thread on the Pixel 11: 29 August 2026 (port blocked, advised against buying), 1 September 2026 (bare-minimum hardware MTE, most CPU-cache acceleration removed), and 2 September 2026 ("around 5% overhead in the standard benchmarks … we've done limited tests"; Android 17 QPR2 Beta 4 force-disables MTE from firmware and using it requires ignoring `arm64.nomte`). GrapheneOS said **Qualcomm currently limits these features to flagship SoCs**, not that a specific 2026 Motorola lacked MTE.

- Binosi, Barzasi, Carminati, Polino, and Zanero, "The Illusion of Randomness," ACM CCS 2024. Also arXiv:2408.15107.
- Apple Platform Security, "Operating system integrity": MIE on "A19 and M5 processors or later". The guide PDF downloaded 15 October 2026 is the **August 2026** edition, not January 2026; the January 2026 citation was stale and the claim survives in the current edition. Computerworld, 29 January 2026, reported an earlier edition. Apple Security Research, "Memory Integrity Enforcement," 9 September 2025, remains the design write-up and still describes third-party opt-in.
- GrapheneOS, 29 August, 1 September, and 2 September 2026: Pixel 11 MTE first looked absent, then bare-minimum hardware, then the firmware force-disable (`arm64.nomte`) and the ~5% asymmetric-mode figure. Not a shipped GrapheneOS device.
- Microsoft Security Blog, 20 September 2022: HVCI "will be enabled by default on all new Windows 11 devices." That is a new-device claim, not a clean-install claim.
- Edge UMCI: Microsoft Edge Dev blog, 23 February 2017, a kernel-enforced User Mode Code Integrity policy "requires DLLs to be Microsoft, Windows Store, or WHQL-signed." CIG is therefore broader than Microsoft-signed only.
- Retpoline: GCC changes for Spectre landed in the GCC 8 tree in January 2018 (Phoronix, 14 January 2018); the Linux kernel series `[PATCH v5 00/12] Retpoline` was submitted 30 January 2018 and is absent from v4.14 and present in v4.15. "Every major OS that month" was wrong, and OpenBSD 6.3 explicitly did not adopt it.
- `MAP_STACK`: the 6.3 release notes say "Prepare for the introduction of MAP_STACK to mmap(2) after 6.3", while the OpenBSD 6.3 `mmap(2)` man page already documents it with a February 2018 date. The functional release is 6.4 per the 6.4 notes.
- PatchGuard: the paper's own dateline is 1 December 2005; the Uninformed volume that carried it is dated 2006.Jan. "Analyzed in Uninformed, Dec 2005" follows the paper.
- Respectre: the grsecurity announcement is dated October 4, 2018; the PRWeb wire copy carries 5 October 2018.
- Microsoft, Peter Waxman, Windows IT Pro Blog, 1 September 2026, "Expanding memory integrity protection across Windows devices." Gradual quality-update rollout beginning October 2026. Does not override an existing off choice. Also enables VBS where required.
- Phoronix, 30 September 2025: the Linux 6.18 hardening merge window starts the `CONFIG_CFI_CLANG` to compiler-neutral `CONFIG_CFI` rename because GCC CFI support was arriving. Linux 6.18 itself was released 30 November 2025. Not treated here as a distro default.
- Alexander Popov, [kernel-hardening-checker](https://github.com/a13xp0p0v/kernel-hardening-checker), formerly kconfig-hardened-check. Recommendations from KSPP, grsecurity's attack-surface cuts, CLIP OS, and GrapheneOS. The README's autodetect example is Ubuntu `6.11.0-1007-oem`. Used here for what that one config actually enabled, not as a survey of every distro. The same author's [Linux Kernel Defence Map](https://github.com/a13xp0p0v/linux-kernel-defence-map) is a relationship map for kernel v6.17, not a date source. Used for merge versions it cites (latent entropy 4.9, STRUCTLEAK 4.11, USERCOPY 4.8, freelist randomization 4.7/4.8, freelist hardening 4.14, `__ro_after_init` 4.6, `DEBUG_LIST` 4.10, PAN 4.3, PXN 3.7/3.19, `VMAP_STACK` 4.9, kernel canary entropy 4.12, `STATIC_USERMODEHELPER` 4.11, BPF blinding 4.7, FORTIFY 4.13). - [Dapper Secure Kernel Patchset Stable](https://github.com/dapperlinux/dapper-secure-kernel-patchset-stable). Last tagged release 4.9.135. The 4.9.135 note misspells retpoline as "Reptoline". A forward port of public grsecurity 4.9.24, not the commercial tree.
- OpenBSD 3.3 release notes (1 May 2003): W^X on sparc, sparc64, alpha, and hppa. The notes say i386 and powerpc do not support W^X in 3.3. OpenBSD 3.4 release notes (1 Nov 2003): i386 W^X via the CS limit, and library-order randomization. OpenBSD 5.3 release notes (1 May 2013): SMEP and SMAP support on i386 and amd64. OpenBSD 5.9 release notes (29 Mar 2016): `tame(2)` renamed `pledge(2)`. OpenBSD 6.3 release notes (15 Apr 2018): Meltdown mitigation, arm64 KPTI, arm BTB flush, thread-stack gap, `pool_get` perturbation, `execpromises`. OpenBSD errata, 26 August 2017, for 6.0 and 6.1: SMAP enforcement could be bypassed.
- PaX Team, grsecurity forums topic 3046, 7 September 2012: "Intel implements UDEREF equivalent 6 years after PaX." PaX Team comment on LWN, 4 October 2012: "by then 7-year-old UDEREF/i386 feature in PaX." Both are their own estimate of elapsed time and they differ by a year.
- Microsoft Learn, *Exploit protection reference*, and *PROCESS_MITIGATION_DYNAMIC_CODE_POLICY* (updated 22 February 2024). - PaX Team slides (SSTIC / Latinoware, and the 2022 BlueHat note): MPROTECT in 2000, SEGMEXEC in 2002, KERNEXEC in 2003. The `mprotect.txt` timestamp of 4 November 2003 is the document date. - kernelnewbies, Linux 4.13 (3 September 2017): `CONFIG_FORTIFY_SOURCE`, Daniel Micay, and the randstruct plugin, commit `313dd1b6`. kernelnewbies 4.12 does not list randstruct. - NetBSD 8.0 announcement, 17 July 2018: PaX MPROTECT and PaX ASLR on by default for the listed architectures, and SMAP support. SMEP is not in that announcement.
- NetBSD: the 8.0 announcement is at https://www.netbsd.org/releases/formal-8/NetBSD-8.0.html (the `/announcements/8.0/announce.html` path 404s). A case-insensitive search of the whole page for `SMEP|SMAP` returns exactly one hit, `SMAP support.`, and zero for SMEP. The `netbsd-8` branch configs independently reproduce the MPROTECT/ASLR architecture split. NetBSD 2.0 (9 December 2004) and 5.0 (29 April 2009) dates from https://www.netbsd.org/releases/formal.html and are confirmed by the 2.0 and 5.0 announcement pages and by the 2.0 change log entry "Add support for non-executable mappings for many platforms". ASLR default-on: christos, `b80ac22793b4`, 10 April 2016, flipping `PAX_ASLR=0` to `=1` in `sys/arch/amd64/conf/GENERIC`; `netbsd-7` still carries `PAX_ASLR=0`. NetBSD has **no `execstack(3)` man page** and never did. Neither pledge nor RETGUARD: zero matches for those names in the trunk `lib/libc` tree, and `security(7)` lists PaX ASLR, PaX MPROTECT, PaX SegvGuard, SSP, FORTIFY_SOURCE and NULL-deref protection but no pledge and no RETGUARD. KASLR backport to 8.x never happened. NetBSD 5.0 announcement, 29 April 2009: userland ASLR. Maxime Villard, 12 October 2017: amd64 KASLR in NetBSD-current.
